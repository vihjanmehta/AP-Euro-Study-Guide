Theme 1 — Interaction of Europe and the World (INT)

Beginning in the 15th century, European nations sent explorers beyond the Mediterranean, establishing new shipping routes, trading stations, and colonies. These explorations created complex trade systems that profoundly affected European prosperity, consumption patterns, and national rivalries. Cross-cultural influence flowed in both directions.

Period 1: 1450–1648
  • Black Death (1346–1353) — demographic collapse reshaping European society
  • Portuguese Empire — Henry the Navigator, Vasco da Gama, Magellan
  • Spanish Empire — Ferdinand/Isabella, Charles V, Philip II
  • Bullionism and the "Favorable Balance of Trade" → Mercantilism; precious metals (silver, gold)
  • Columbian Exchange and Triangular Trade
    • New foods (tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, corn) transform diets of China, India, Europe
    • Old World diseases devastate indigenous American populations
  • Dutch East India Company (VOC — Indonesia)
  • Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) — divides New World between Spain and Portugal
  • Global Jesuit Missions (Asia, Americas)
Period 2: 1648–1815
  • French Mercantilism under Jean-Baptiste Colbert
  • Seven Years' War (1756–63) — first global war (Frederick the Great); Britain vs. France worldwide
  • American Revolution (1776–83)
  • Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) — Toussaint L'Ouverture leads slave revolt; inspires abolition debate
  • Slave Trade — transatlantic labor system; "Middle Passage"; 16th–19th c.; abolitionists challenge it via Enlightenment ideology
  • East India Companies (British, Dutch) — mercantile colonialism in India
Period 3: 1815–1914
  • Latin American Wars of Independence (1820s–1830s)
  • New Imperialism and "White Man's Burden" — Social Darwinism justifies empire
  • "Anglobalization" — British domination in India, China, Africa, Middle East (Victorian Era c. 1840–1900)
    • British Imperialism → Indian Uprisings (1857–1947)
    • British and French in Africa (1895)
  • Berlin Conference (1884–1885) and Scramble for Africa — divides continent among European powers
  • French Algeria and Indochina
  • Fashoda Incident (1898) and Entente Cordiale (1904) — alliance between France and Britain
  • Leopold II and Belgian Congo — brutal exploitation
  • Sepoy Mutiny (1857) and Boxer Rebellion (1900)
  • Opium Wars → British vs. China, 1830s–1860s; Treaty of Nanjing (1842); Hong Kong to Britain
  • Russo-Japanese War (1905) — first Asian power defeats European nation
Period 4: 1914–2001
  • World War I — Sykes-Picot Agreement + Mandates reshape Middle East
  • World War II
  • Middle Eastern Mandates and Turkey (post-WWI settlements)
  • Israel (1948 founding)
  • Partition of India (1947)
  • Petroleum Politics → OPEC, Petrodollar
  • Algerian Independence (1954–62)
  • Korean and Vietnam Wars (1950–75)
  • Soviet Union and Afghanistan (1980s)
  • Decolonization and Globalization — 20th-century collapse of European empires
Essential Questions
  • Why have Europeans sought contact and interaction with other parts of the world?
  • What political, technological, and intellectual developments enabled European contact and interaction?
  • How have encounters between Europe and the world shaped European culture, politics, and society?
  • What impact has contact with Europe had on non-European societies?
Theme 2 — Economic and Commercial Developments (ECD)

In the centuries after 1450, Europe gradually came to dominate a global commercial network. The commercial revolution of the 17th and 18th centuries involved joint-stock companies, widely capitalized banks, and triangular trade — all supporting an emerging money economy. Mercantilism, the Industrial Revolution, and capitalism each transformed European economic life.

Period 1: 1450–1648
  • Feudalism and Serfdom — end in England by 1570s (Elizabeth I); Dutch by 1580s; France by 1789; Italy/Spain by 1804–1815 (Napoleonic Code); Austria/Prussia revolutions of 1848; Russia 1861 (Alexander II)
  • Renaissance Italy — banking families (Medicis), early capitalism
  • German Peasants' War (1524–25) — social upheaval amid Reformation
  • Bullionism — wealth = precious metals; drove exploration and colonization
  • Inflation in the Spanish Empire — Price Revolution from influx of New World silver
  • Dutch Golden Age (c. 1600–1700) — Amsterdam as financial center; VOC; joint-stock companies
  • Anabaptist Communalism
  • Baroque vs. Dutch Reform Art/Architecture — reflecting religious and economic divergence
Period 2: 1648–1815
  • Louis XIV and Versailles — Colbert's mercantilist policies; domestic manufacturing protection
  • Peter the Great and "Westernization" — modernizing Russia along European economic lines
  • Pugachev's Rebellion (1773–74) — Russian peasant uprising against Catherine
  • Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) — laissez-faire capitalism; "invisible hand"
  • Thomas Malthus's "Essay on Population" (1798) — population vs. food supply; Malthusian trap
  • Women's March (1789) — bread riots in Paris precede Revolution
  • Sans-Culottes — urban workers driving radical phase of French Revolution
  • Agricultural Revolution and Proto-Industrialization — "putting-out system," cottage industry; early Britain (1660s) → most of Europe (1700s)
  • Napoleon's Continental System — economic blockade of Britain
Period 3: 1815–1914
  • Liberalism (e.g., Anti-Corn-Law League) — free trade ideology
  • Trade Unionism and Socialism (e.g., Chartism, Utopianism)
  • Communism (e.g., Marxism, Communards) — revolutionary class struggle
  • Industrial Revolution and Second Industrial Revolution (steel, chemicals, electricity)
  • Revolutions of 1830 and 1848 in France
  • Bourgeoisie and Proletariat — new class structure created by capitalism
  • Haussmannization and Urban Renewal — Haussmann rebuilds Paris; sanitizes and controls
  • Zollverein — German customs union; economic precursor to political unification
  • Scramble for Africa and China — economic imperatives driving New Imperialism
Period 4: 1914–2001
  • "War Guilt Clause" (Treaty of Versailles) — Article 231; reparations cripple Weimar economy
  • Great Depression (1929–39) — mass unemployment; bank collapses; authoritarianism rises
  • John Maynard Keynes — Keynesian economics; government deficit spending to stimulate growth
  • Soviet Communism — command economy; collectivization; Five-Year Plans
  • Liquidation of the kulaks (1929–33) — Stalin's war on peasant landowners
  • Fascism — corporate state; private ownership under state direction
  • Marshall Plan (1948) — US aid rebuilds Western European economies
  • Berlin Wall (1961–1989) — physical symbol of economic/ideological divide
  • Welfare State — cradle-to-grave social provisions; NHS (UK); social democracies
  • European Union / European Central Bank — common currency (Euro); economic integration
Essential Questions
  • How has capitalism developed as an economic system?
  • How has the organization of society changed as a result of capitalism?
  • What were the causes and consequences of economic and social inequity?
  • How did individuals, groups, and the state respond to economic and social inequality?
Key Economic Concepts
Mercantilism — wealth is finite; exports must exceed imports; maximize bullion reserves; promoted commercial competition and warfare overseas. Key minister: Colbert (France).
Capitalism / Adam Smith — wealth can expand through free markets ("invisible hand"); laissez-faire; supply/demand; opposed to mercantilism.
Marxism — dialectical materialism; bourgeoisie exploits proletariat; class struggle leads to revolution; communism = classless society; applied in USSR under Lenin/Stalin.
Keynesianism — government spending during recession; deficit spending to avoid Depression-era collapse; basis of postwar welfare state.
Theme 3 — Cultural and Intellectual Developments (CID)

Starting in the 15th century, European thinkers developed new methods for arriving at objective truth, gradually moving from religious authority to empiricism and reason. Science, philosophy, art, and literature all reflected these shifts — from Renaissance Humanism through the Scientific Revolution, Enlightenment, Romanticism, and into 20th-century Modernism.

Period 1: 1450–1648
  • Roman Catholic Church — dominant arbiter of truth and morality; challenged by reformers
  • Reformation — Luther (1517), Calvin, Zwingli, Henry VIII; Protestant alternatives to Rome
  • Lutheranism, Calvinism/Puritanism, and Anabaptism — competing Protestant branches
  • Witches and Popular ("Folk") Superstitions — Church prosecutes ~50,000 alleged witches (16th–17th c.)
  • Council of Trent and Catholic Reformation — Church corrects abuses; Jesuits educate
  • Renaissance Art, Geometry, and Science — Leonardo da Vinci; humanist naturalism
  • Scientific Revolution — Copernicus (heliocentric), Galileo (telescope, inertia), Francis Bacon (inductive method)
  • Rationalism — Descartes: "Cogito ergo sum"; deductive reasoning; mind-body dualism
  • Natural Philosophy — early scientists studying physical world through observation
Period 2: 1648–1815
  • "State of Nature" — Hobbes (brutish, short), Locke (peaceful), Rousseau (noble savage)
  • Enlightenment, Rationalism, and Secularism — reason applied to politics, society, religion
  • Neoclassicism — art and architecture reflecting Greek/Roman ideals; civic virtue
  • Newtonian Physics — universal laws of gravity; clockwork universe
  • Diderot's Encyclopédie — codifies all knowledge; challenges Church authority
  • Salons — intellectual gathering places run by noblewomen; spread Enlightenment ideas
  • General Will (Rousseau's Social Contract) — sovereignty resides in the people
  • School systems under Enlightened Despots — Frederick, Joseph II promote education
  • Rising literacy in the 18th century — print culture democratizes knowledge
Period 3: 1815–1914
  • Conservatism vs. Liberalism vs. Socialism — three dominant 19th-c. ideologies
  • Anarchism — Proudhon, Bakunin; reject all state authority
  • Romanticism, Realism — reaction against Enlightenment rationalism; emotion, nature, national folk traditions
  • Impressionism — Monet, Renoir; reject academic painting; capture light/moment
  • Nietzschean philosophy (Thus Spoke Zarathustra) — "God is dead"; will to power; Superman
  • Nationalism — defining cultural/artistic movement; inspires unification and imperialism
  • Natural Selection (Darwinism), Positivism, and Social Darwinism — apply science to society
  • "New Science" (e.g., Faraday, Curies, Pasteur) — chemistry, electricity, germ theory
  • Pan-Slavism, Pan-Germanism — ethnic-nationalist intellectual movements
Period 4: 1914–2001
  • Modernism vs. Fascist Art — abstraction vs. propaganda; degeneracy campaigns in Nazi Germany
  • Aryan Race — Nazi pseudo-scientific racism
  • Einstein's Theory of Relativity — shatters Newtonian certainty; observer matters
  • Leninist-Trotskyite "Global Communism" — spread of revolution worldwide
  • Freudian Psychology — unconscious drives; sexuality; irrational forces in behavior
  • Existentialism — Sartre, Camus; no inherent meaning; we create our own values
  • Post-Modernism — no absolute truths; cultural relativism; deconstruction
  • Second Vatican Council (1963) — Catholic reform; Mass in vernacular; ecumenism
Essential Questions
  • What roles have traditional sources of authority (church and classical antiquity) played in the creation and transmission of knowledge?
  • How and why did Europeans come to rely on the scientific method and reason in place of traditional authorities?
  • How and why did Europeans come to value subjective interpretations of reality?
Theme 4 — States and Other Institutions of Power (SOP)

After 1450, the old ideal of a unified Christendom weakened as sovereign states asserted monopoly over law and institutions. States evolved from absolute monarchies to constitutional governments to totalitarian regimes — with the European state system originating at Westphalia (1648).

Period 1: 1450–1648
  • Valois to Bourbon Monarchy (France) — centralization under Francis I, Henri IV, Louis XIII
  • Tudor to Stuart Monarchy (England) — Henry VIII → Elizabeth I → James I
  • Habsburg Monarchy (Holy Roman Empire, Spain) — Charles V/Philip II spanning Europe
  • Italian city-states (Venice, Florence, Milan) — merchant oligarchies; Renaissance patrons
  • Dutch Republic — merchant oligarchy; Calvinist; first modern republic
  • Roman Catholic Church and the Great Schism (1378–1417) — conciliarist challenge to papal authority
  • German princedoms — fragmented; Peace of Augsburg (1555) gives princes religious choice
  • Bohemia — Protestant rebellion → Thirty Years' War
  • Expansion of Ottoman Empire — Suleiman the Magnificent; Battle of Lepanto (1571)
  • Ivan the Terrible to Russian Empire
Period 2: 1648–1815
  • Cromwell to Stuart to Hanover Monarchy (England) — Interregnum → Restoration → Glorious Revolution → Hanoverian succession
  • Bourbon Monarchy to Republic to Empire (France) — Louis XIV → Revolution → Republic → Napoleon
  • Absolutism and Enlightened Despotism (Prussia, Austria, Russia) — Frederick, Joseph II, Catherine
  • Holy Roman Empire → German Confederation (dissolved 1806 by Napoleon)
  • Austrian Empire (Charles VI – Joseph II) — pragmatic sanction; Enlightened reforms
  • Prussian Duchy to Kingdom (Frederick William – Frederick the Great) — military state
  • Russian Empire (Peter the Great – Catherine the Great) — westernization; expansion
  • Polish Partition (1772, 1793, 1795) — erased from map by Russia, Prussia, Austria
  • Continental System and the Bonapartes — Napoleon reshapes European map
  • Concert of Europe (Congress of Vienna, 1815) — Metternich system; balance of power
Period 3: 1815–1914
  • Cavour/Garibaldi and Italian Unification (1848–71) — Risorgimento
  • Bismarck and German Unification (1848–71) — Realpolitik; blood and iron; Kulturkampf
  • "Sick Man of Europe" and Congress of Berlin (1878) — Ottoman decline; Balkans divided
  • Pan-Hellenism and Greek Independence
  • Belgian and Serbian Independence
  • Emancipation of the Serfs in Russia (1861) — Alexander II
  • Russian Empire (Alexander II – Nicholas II) — Reform → Revolution → Collapse
  • British Empire, Reform Bills, and Party Politics (Whigs/Tories → Liberals/Conservatives/Labour)
  • Resistance to British Empire (Ireland, India, China)
  • Bourbon Restoration to Third Republic (France) — 1830 revolution → Louis Philippe → 1848 → Napoleon III → Paris Commune → Republic
Period 4: 1914–2001
  • Fascist Italy (1922–45) — Mussolini; corporate state; Lateran Pact (1929)
  • Nazi Germany (1933–45) — Hitler; totalitarianism; Holocaust
  • Soviet Union (1917–1991) — Bolsheviks → Stalin → Khrushchev → Brezhnev → Gorbachev
  • Collapse of former empires (WWI):
    • Austria-Hungary → Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, etc.
    • Ottoman → Turkey, Syria, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel
  • Soviet Satellite States (Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary)
  • Yugoslavia and Titoism — non-aligned; communist but independent of USSR
  • European Union — from ECSC (1951) to EEC (1957) to EU (1993)
  • German Reunification (1989–90)
  • Collapse of Soviet Union → Russia, Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia, Ukraine, Belarus, etc.
  • Yugoslav Wars (1991–95) — genocide; NATO intervention
Essential Questions
  • What forms have European governments taken, and how have these changed over time?
  • In what ways and why have European governments moved toward or reacted against representative and democratic principles?
Theme 5 — Social Organization and Development (SOC)

Early modern society was divided into three estates: clergy, nobility, and commoners (including merchants, townspeople, and the overwhelming peasant majority). Commercial revolution, industrialization, and political revolutions progressively transformed these social hierarchies. The status of women, family structure, and class identity all shifted dramatically across the period.

Period 1: 1450–1648 — Key Figures
  • Jan Hus and John Wycliffe — proto-reformers; challenge Church corruption
  • Civic Humanism (Pico della Mirandola, Machiavelli, Castiglione) — man as master of own destiny
  • Italian Renaissance artists — portraiture, Greco-Roman classicism

Theme 6 — National and European Identity (NEI)

Prior to modern nation-states, European life centered on local and personal loyalties. Modern nationalism arose out of Enlightenment social contract theory combined with the effort to realize those ideals during the French Revolution. By the 19th century, nationalism became one of the most powerful ideologies of change — leading to unifications, imperialism, and ultimately two world wars.

Period 1: 1450–1648 — Religion as primary identity
  • Reconquista and Ferdinand/Isabella — Catholic identity vs. Muslim/Jewish "other"
  • Hundred Years' War and Joan of Arc — proto-national French consciousness
  • Great Schism (1378–1417) — challenges to unified Christian identity
  • Reformation and Catholic Reformation — religious identity fractures Europe
  • Zwingli's Zurich and Calvin's Geneva — city-states as Protestant models
  • Henry VIII and Anglican Church — national church breaks from Rome
  • Battle of Lepanto against Ottomans (1571) — "Christian Europe" vs. Islam
  • Dutch Golden Age — Calvinist merchant republic as distinct identity
  • French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) — Catholic vs. Huguenot; resolved Edict of Nantes
  • Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) → creates nation-states (Dutch Republic, Switzerland)
Period 2: 1648–1815 — State-building; early nationalism
  • Trade Wars (England vs. Netherlands, England vs. France, England vs. Spain)
  • War of Austrian Succession (Prussia/France vs. Austria/England)
  • Wars of Louis XIV (e.g., Spanish Succession) — French hegemony vs. balance of power
  • Seven Years' War (Prussia/England vs. Austria/France) — global dimension
  • Enlightened Despotism — nationalism as a tool of ruling elites
  • Partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795) — nationalism crushed by great powers
  • Napoleon's Continental System — French imperial nationalism
  • Napoleon and Nationalism (German Confederation, Italy, Spain, Russia) — nationalism spreads as reaction to French conquest
  • Concert of Europe (Congress of Vienna) — conservative attempt to suppress nationalism
Period 3: 1815–1914 — Age of nationalism

  • Austro-Prussian War (1866)
  • Ausgleich (Compromise: Austria-Hungary, 1867)
  • Unification of Germany (1871)
Period 4: 1914–2001 — Nationalism's deadly consequences
  • Militarism, Alliances, Nationalism, Imperialism → WWI (MAIN causes)
  • Fascism vs. Communism — competing nationalisms and internationalism
  • Totalitarian States (Spain, Soviet Union, Germany, Italy)

  • European Economic Community → European Union — post-nationalist integration
  • New Nationalist Fronts and challenges to EU — Brexit; far-right parties

Social History — Marriage, Family, Structure & Health

AP Euro social history spans changes in family structure, marriage patterns, social class, slavery, nutrition, and health across the centuries. These topics frequently appear in DBQ documents and SAQ prompts.

Late Middle Ages
  • Marriages arranged for economic reasons; nuclear family; divorce nonexistent; prostitution in urban areas
  • Average age for men: mid-late 20s; for women: 20 years old
  • Church encouraged cult of paternal care; many couples didn't observe church regulations
  • Manners shaped men to please women; relative sexual equality
16th–17th Century
  • Population grew faster than rural areas; cities grew (until 1650)
  • Average age for marriage: 27 for men; 25 for women
  • Low rate of illegitimate births; increased infanticide
  • Dramatic population growth until 1650; growth slows to 1750
18th Century
  • Growth of Cottage Industry; nuclear family; marriages based more on romance
  • Average age for marriage: late 20s or later; takes longer to be ready economically
  • Many women don't marry ("spinsters")
  • Illegitimate birth explosion (1750–1850); Foundling hospitals created
  • Young people increasingly worked away from home in towns → "Spare the rod, spoil the child" challenged by Enlightenment humanism
19th Century
  • Ideal of romantic love becomes important; fewer children per family; more investment in each child
  • Middle class: consider economic reasons for marriage; many men marry late; women closely monitored; sexual double standard
  • Rate of illegitimacy declined after 1850 in working classes
  • Freud: early childhood is vital
  • Lower class children less dependent on parents financially than middle class children
20th Century
  • Baby Boom after WWII
  • Women having children earlier and fewer (about 2.0); middle class children less economically dependent on parents
Late Middle Ages — Status of Women
  • Status of upper-class women better than in the next two centuries
16th–17th Century
  • Status of upper-class women declines in Renaissance
  • Most women not affected by Renaissance; educated women allowed involvement but subservient to men

Women in European History

The status, opportunities, and political roles of women shifted significantly across AP Euro's periods. Key debate: Joan Kelly's "Did Women Have a Renaissance?" (answer: largely no for most women). Know how major events — Reformation, Enlightenment, French Revolution, industrialization, WWI, WWII — affected women's lives.

I. Renaissance
Wealthy Women
  • Querelles des Femmes ("The Problem of Women") — debate over women's nature and proper role; began with Christine de Pisan (14th c.); lasted 600 years
  • Increased access to education; lost some status compared to Middle Ages; to be "ornaments" to their husbands
  • Important Renaissance noblewomen at court: Christine de Pisan, Isabella d'Este (Mantua), Artemesia Gentilleschi (famous for "Judith" pictures)
  • Important Female Rulers: Caterina Sforza, Isabella I, Mary Tudor, Elizabeth I, Catherine de Médicis
Women in General
  • Status did not change much compared to Middle Ages
  • European Family Pattern: nuclear family (poor → unable to support extended families); wealthier people → extended families
  • Based on economic considerations; not love; dowries very important in wealthy families
  • Average age for women: <20 (men: mid-late 20s); Italy — age gap much larger than N. Europe
  • Increased infanticide and abandonment (2/3 of abandoned babies were girls)
  • Low rate of illegitimate births; dramatic population growth until 1650
  • Sexual double standard; women were to remain chaste; men could "sow wild oats"
  • Persecution of alleged witches — official RCC dogma in 1484; large number were older women
  • Joan Kelly: Did Women Have a Renaissance? — No, for most women
II. Reformation
  • Protestant women: occupation in the home caring for family
    • Protestant churches had greater official control over marriage
    • Marriage became more companionate — Luther and Katerina von Bora as model of husband/helpmate
    • Increased women's literacy → needed to read Bible and teach children
    • Lost opportunities in church service that Catholic women enjoyed
  • Catholic women:
    • Continued to enjoy opportunities in Church through religious orders
    • Teresa de Avila; Angela Merici (Ursuline Order)
III. 18th Century (Agricultural Revolution & Industrial Revolution)
  • Agricultural Revolution (Enclosure):
    • Women had fewer opportunities to profit from common land work
    • Women increasingly worked away from home in towns/cities; most work was domestic
    • Social consequences: more autonomy, save for own dowries, slight more choice in marriage partners
  • Growth of cottage industry — women increasingly home-working; young women sent away to work
  • Industrial Revolution — large numbers of women work in factories (late 18th c. England); Family wage economy (Factory Act 1833 limits child labor)
  • Marriage — based more on romance; average age late 20s or later; large population of "spinsters"
  • Decrease in witch hunts — scientific ideas + decline of RCC political power
  • Important Female Rulers: Catherine the Great, Maria Teresa
  • Women in the Enlightenment:
    • Émilie du Châtelet — translated Newton's Principia (Voltaire's mistress)
    • Salons: Madame de Geoffrin, Louise de Warens, Germaine de Staël, Jeanne Rolan
    • Rousseau's Émile (1762): separate education for boys and girls; domestic roles for women
    • Catharine Macaulay, Letters on Education (1787); Hannah More, "Bluestockings"
    • Enlightenment ideology largely did not apply freedom/equality to women; French Revolution leaders repressed women's political activities
IV. Women in the French Revolution
  • Bread riots; March on Versailles (October Days)
  • Olympe de Gouges: The Rights of Woman (1791)
  • Mary Wollstonecraft: Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792)
  • Participation with the Sans Culottes (Society of Revolutionary Republican Women)
  • Closing of women's political clubs by the National Convention — leaders identified women with debauchery and effete style of the Ancien Régime
  • Charlotte Corday; Salons (Roland, Girondins); Victims of Reign of Terror: De Gouges, Roland
  • Napoleonic France: Civil Code reasserted Old Regime's patriarchal system; women = legal incompetents; state paternalism; Madame de Staël criticized Napoleon's regime
V. 19th Century
  • Industrial Revolution — large numbers of women in factories
    • After 1850: separate spheres — men in factories; women at home
    • Protective legislation drove women out of certain employment
  • Marriage — Ideal of romantic love; fewer children; middle class monitor women closely; Freud: early childhood vital
  • Status — ideology of domesticity; Victorian ideal; "cult of domesticity"; by late 19th c. middle-class women began organizing
  • Romanticism: George Sand (Amandine-Aurore Dupin); Realism: George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans)
  • Women played major role in social reforms:
    • Catholic orders organized schools and hospitals; Temperance movement
    • Pacifism: Bertha von Suttner's Lay Down Your Arms (1889)
    • Active in socialist movement: Owenites, Emma Martin, Flora Tristan
    • Modernism in W. Europe — "New Woman": drop in birth rates; Ellen Key, Nelly Roussel, Marguerite Durand (La Fronde)
VI. Female Suffrage
  • Finland — first to grant female suffrage (1906)
  • Other countries by 1920: Norway, Denmark, Iceland, Netherlands, Russia, Czechoslovakia, Britain, Germany, Austria
  • England:
    • John Stuart Mill, The Subjection of Women (1869) — predominantly middle-class movement
    • Millicent Garrett Fawcett — moderate suffragists
    • Emmeline Pankhurst (Women's Social and Political Union) — militant tactics: violence, bombings, property destruction, picketing Parliament
    • Representation of the People Act 1918 (age 30+); 1928 (age 21+)
VII. 20th Century
  • Russia — equality after Revolution (in theory): voting, education, jobs, no double standard; compare to French Revolution role
  • Traditional and oppressed role in Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany — encouraged to have many children; denied high-paying jobs
  • Post-WWI — several countries passed repressive reproductive legislation despite suffrage victories
  • Post-WWII:

Enlightened Despots used Enlightenment ideas to strengthen traditional monarchical authority, not undermine it. How much did it actually change political and social realities?

Evidence for CHALLENGE (Enlightenment challenged tradition)

  • Natural rights philosophy (Locke) — challenged divine right; sovereignty belongs to people
  • Rousseau's General Will — popular sovereignty; government accountable to citizens
  • Voltaire's anticlerical satire — attacked Church corruption and intolerance
  • Diderot's Encyclopédie — undermined Church intellectual monopoly
  • Mary Wollstonecraft — challenged patriarchal structure; equal rights for women
  • American Revolution (1776) and French Revolution (1789)
  • Declaration of Rights of Man (1789) — liberty, equality, fraternity
  • Napoleon's Civil Code — legal equality; abolished feudal privileges
  • Abolition movement — natural rights applied to slavery

Evidence for CONTINUITY (Enlightenment had limits)

  • Enlightened Despots (Frederick the Great, Catherine the Great, Joseph II) — used Enlightenment to justify and strengthen absolute monarchy
  • Women excluded from Revolution's promises; Napoleon's Code reduced women to legal incompetents
  • Aristocracy survived across most of Europe
  • Serfdom persisted in Eastern Europe despite Enlightenment
  • Slave trade continued through the 18th century
  • Congress of Vienna (1815) — restored monarchies; suppressed liberal gains
  • Church remained powerful in Spain, Italy, Austria, Russia

Suggested Thesis Direction

While the Enlightenment fundamentally challenged political authority and inspired the French Revolution, its challenge to traditional social structures was substantially limited — women, enslaved peoples, and Eastern European peasants remained subordinate, and Enlightened Despots co-opted Enlightenment ideas to reinforce rather than dismantle monarchical authority.
